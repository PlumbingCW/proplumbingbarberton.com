Water System Leak Detection in Barberton, OH

You can often tell if your home has a leak with a water meter and a few minutes. Four simple checks narrow it down, and a plumber's equipment finds what the tests suggest.

Call (855) 575-2890

By the Barberton Pro Plumbing team. Updated 2026.

Start with the meter test

Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including the ice maker and the dishwasher. Find your water meter, which is usually in the basement or a utility area, and look at the small triangle or dial that spins when water flows.

If it moves with everything off, water is going somewhere. Note the reading, wait an hour without using water and look again. A changed reading confirms a leak.

Toilets are the usual suspect

A leaking flapper can waste a surprising amount of water without a sound. Drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. If color shows in the bowl, the flapper needs replacing.

A cheap part fixes it. If the flapper is fine and the meter still moves, the leak is in a supply line, a fixture or a buried pipe.

Clues that point to hidden leaks

Warm spots on a floor can mean a hot water line leaking under it. A musty smell, bubbling paint or a stain that grows after you run water are further clues. A sound of running water in a quiet house is another.

Take photos of what you find and note when each clue appears. That helps a plumber narrow the search, and our leak detection team uses acoustic gear and thermal imaging to find the exact spot.

When to call a plumber

Call if the meter moves with everything off, if you see damage that grows or if you cannot find the source. Early detection limits damage to drywall, framing and insulation.

If a pipe has burst or water is actively flowing, shut off the main and follow the steps on our burst pipe repair page. If the water is groundwater, read about basement waterproofing.

Reading the meter correctly

Most meters have a sweep hand or a numeric register in cubic feet or gallons, plus a small low-flow indicator, often a triangle or gear, that spins with even a trickle. Check the label on the meter face to see the units.

Take a photo of the reading before and after your wait. Photos remove doubt and are useful if you need to discuss the bill with the utility.

Leaks under floors and behind walls

A leak in a concealed line can run for weeks before it shows. Warm patches on a floor, a musty closet or a stain that spreads with no rain are the usual signs. Ceilings below bathrooms are another place to look.

A plumber uses acoustic equipment to hear the leak and a thermal camera to see temperature differences. The goal is to open as small a section as possible.

What a leak costs you

A faucet that drips once per second wastes thousands of gallons a year. A running toilet can waste far more. Even a modest leak adds up on a bill and can damage materials around it.

Fixing a small leak early is nearly always cheaper than repairing the damage it causes. That is the real reason to run the meter test twice a year.

A simple pressure gauge test

Screw a pressure gauge onto an outdoor hose bib with all fixtures off. Note the reading and check it again after an hour. A steady needle means the house holds pressure, while a falling needle points to a leak.

The test works best when the water heater and appliances are quiet. A small drop can come from thermal expansion, so repeat it before you conclude.

What to tell the plumber

Share your meter test results, photos of stains, when the bill changed and whether you have a sprinkler or pool line. Mention the age of the house and any plumbing work done in the past.

That history shortens the search and lowers the cost of finding the leak.

What the EPA says about leaks

According to the EPA, the average home loses nearly 10,000 gallons of water a year to leaks. The meter test in this guide is the same first step a plumber uses.
