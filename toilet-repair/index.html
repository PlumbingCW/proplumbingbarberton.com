Overflowing toilet: stop it now

If the bowl is rising, reach behind the toilet and turn the small oval handle on the supply valve clockwise to stop the refill. Our dispatch is open 24/7, and while you wait you can lift the tank lid and raise the float to stop water entering the bowl.

Mop up quickly, because water seeps into subfloors. If the toilet has overflowed more than once, the clog may be beyond the trap, and our drain cleaning team can clear the line.

Common toilet problems

Most toilet faults come from a handful of cheap parts.

  • ✓Running water that never stops after a flush
  • ✓A phantom flush every few hours
  • ✓Weak flush or repeated clogs
  • ✓Water around the base of the toilet
  • ✓A rocking toilet that moves on the floor
  • ✓A tank that sweats or leaks at the bolts

Hard water and toilet parts

Barberton's latest water quality report lists hardness at about 170 mg/L, so minerals build on fill valves and flush valve seals. A flapper that looks fine can still leak if scale keeps it from seating.

That is why the same toilet may need a fill valve in five years in hard water but last longer on a softened line. If you also have a softening question, our water softener page covers it.

Installing a new toilet

A replacement is a one-visit job in most bathrooms.

  1. 1

    Shut off and drain

    We close the supply, flush the tank and remove the old toilet.

  2. 2

    Inspect the flange

    We check the closet flange for damage and the floor for rot.

  3. 3

    Set the new toilet

    We fit a new wax ring or gasket, set the bowl and tighten the bolts evenly.

  4. 4

    Connect and test

    We connect a new supply line, check for leaks and flush several times.

Most modern toilets use 1.28 gallons per flush or less, which cuts water use compared with older models. We help you pick a size and rough-in that fits your bathroom.

When to replace, not repair

A cracked bowl or tank, a flange that has failed, or a toilet that keeps clogging despite clean pipes is a replacement case. Repeated repairs on a very old unit cost more than the toilet.

If you are remodeling, plan the install with our plumbing installation team so the water and waste connections are right the first time.

Picking a toilet that fits your bathroom

Measure the rough-in, which is the distance from the finished wall to the center of the drain. Most toilets are built for a 12 inch rough-in, but older bathrooms can differ, and a mismatch means a toilet that sits too close to or too far from the wall.

Bowl shape matters too. An elongated bowl is more comfortable for many adults, while a round bowl saves a couple of inches in a small bath. Ask for a model with a fully glazed trapway, because it resists clogs better than a rough one.

Water-saving standards for toilets

The EPA WaterSense label requires toilets to use 1.28 gallons per flush or less. If your old toilet is a repeated repair, a labeled replacement can lower the water bill.

A toilet that runs after flushing is often a worn flapper, and the fix costs little. Replacing the whole toilet is not always the best first step.
